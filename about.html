Hi. We're the bees.

What this is, why it exists, and how to reach an actual person.

The short version

HIVV is a network of ordinary computers that share AI capacity. You ask a question; the network finds a machine to answer it — sometimes yours, sometimes someone else's, sometimes a small model running in somebody's spare bedroom in a different country.

It is pronounced HIVE. Like a beehive. Not like the virus. This has caused more confusion than any technical decision we have ever made, and we have made some strange ones.

The point is not to build a bigger AI. The point is to make sure the thing answering you is legible — you can see which machine replied, what it did with your request, and whether you allowed it. That last part is the whole reason this project exists.

Why not just use ChatGPT?

You can. Genuinely — nobody here is going to pretend otherwise. If a big hosted model is what you need, use it.

HIVV is for the moment when you want to know where your words went. Which machine read them. Whether it kept them. Whether you said yes to that. When the answer comes back you get a receipt, not a vibe.

Also: it works offline. A plain git clone boots with no database, no internet and no secrets. Your village, your clinic, your basement — still functional. That is not a nice-to-have. That is the point.

What we're actually for

The benchmark is on the Purpose page, and it is short enough to say here:

Does this help all life on Earth?

Health, clean water, food, knowledge, wisdom, working offline, and a bit of fun. If a feature moves none of those, we do not build it — or we flag it publicly and say why.

Honest status: knowledge, wisdom and offline are genuinely served. Health, water and food are named but not yet built. That gap is flagged, not hidden.

How to report something well

  1. What you did — the exact page or command.
  2. What you expected — in one sentence.
  3. What happened instead — copy the error, don't paraphrase it.
  4. Where — browser, phone, or terminal.

That is genuinely all we need. You do not need to diagnose it. You do not need to know what a gateway is. Finding the bug is our job; finding it early is yours, and you already did that part by reporting it.

From inside the product you can also run hivv bug --client X --node Y, or hivv problems to see what is already known.

What happens when you tell us

  1. A human reads it. Not a triage bot.
  2. You get an answer — fixed, known, or "here is why not."
  3. It gets recorded where others can see it, so the same thing doesn't happen twice.

"We'll get to it" is not an answer. If something is broken and we are not fixing it, we will tell you that plainly and say why.

Serious safety issue? Text. It goes to a phone that a person carries.

The deeper reason any of this is worth doing

Something strange is happening with AI right now. The most capable systems in the world are being built by a handful of very large companies, and most people who will depend on them will never see the inside of one, never meet anyone who runs one, and never get a straight answer about what happens to the things they type into them.

That is not a conspiracy. It is just what happens when nobody designs for legibility. And legibility turns out to be the whole thing. A system you cannot inspect is a system you have to trust, and asking people to trust things they cannot check is how you get a world full of people who are right to be anxious.

So: receipts instead of vibes. Names instead of "the AI." Permission before action, and revocation after. Machines you can point at. A network where the interesting question is not how smart is it but can you see what it just did.

Technology should adapt to humans. Humans should not have to become engineers to use technology.

That is the whole project. Everything else — the bees, the honey, the leaderboard, the extremely serious governance dashboard with a mascot on it — is just how we chose to build it. 🐝
